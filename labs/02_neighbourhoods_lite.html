    1 Task 1 · Opposites as neighbours

    Your task: check on the map whether opposites, such as increase and decrease, are close neighbours.

    Predict: will increase and decrease be close neighbours, and will true and false? Then check.

    Look up effective. Then compare increase with decrease and true with false. What surprises you?

    Compare with the model answer

    The closest neighbour of effective is ineffective (62), ahead of increase (54). Compared with each other, increase and decrease have a closeness of 88, the highest on the whole map, and true and false 59. Opposites are used in almost the same sentences ("the intervention was … "), so they end up as neighbours. Being close on the map says nothing about whether two words mean the same, and nothing about which one is true.

    So when you check AI summaries of studies, look closely at the direction of effects.

    2 Task 2 · Lena's words

    Your task: look up praise and reward and find the neighbours that could explain Lena's mix-up.

    Remember sentence 3 of Lena's summary: "Praise and rewards had the strongest effect, while detailed feedback on the task … had only a small effect." Look up praise, then reward, and read their closest neighbours. The higher the closeness, the more similar the sentences in which two words are used. Which neighbours could make this kind of mix-up easy?

    Compare with the model answer

    Across all 64 words, the closest neighbour of praise is joy (59). Among the feedback words, it is its opposite, criticism (58), followed by reward (48). Words for kinds of feedback, and their opposites, fill the same slots in sentences about what works, so they may be easy to swap. The map has single words only, so it cannot test the phrase detailed feedback on the task itself. This is a plausible explanation for Lena's sentence 3, not proof.

    Before you go on

    Recall: In one sentence: which of Lena's sentences does this part explain, and how?

    Your answer so far

    Lena's puzzle, part 1 of 3 (Part A): Words used in similar sentences are neighbours, even opposites. In a sentence about which feedback works best, such words fill the same slots, so naming the wrong kind of feedback can be about as likely as the correct one. That is a plausible explanation for Lena's sentence 3.

    About the data. The word lists (300 numbers per word) come from the open English model en_core_web_lg of the spaCy library (version 3.8). These are fixed word vectors, which are a simpler, older form of the embeddings inside large language models: in a chat model the numbers for a word also change with the sentence around it. The flat map was made with the t-SNE method and is only approximate. All numbers were calculated once and stored in this page.
